# Research Basis

Last reviewed: 2026-08-28

This reference records the evidence behind the skill's stable operating rules. It treats the seven user-selected articles as the base and uses supporting sources only to clarify inaccessible pages, trace claims to primary research, or separate evidence from folklore.

## Base Articles

### 1. Stanford HAI: AI Hiring Tools Can Yield Racial Bias and Systemic Rejection

Source: [Stanford Institute for Human-Centered AI, May 26, 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/ai-hiring-tools-can-yield-racial-bias-and-systemic-rejection)

The reported study followed 3.4 million applicants and four million applications across 1,700 postings, 150 employers, and 11 sectors. It found position-level adverse impact affecting Black and Asian applicants and evidence that dependence on a shared screening vendor can produce correlated rejection across employers.

Resume implications:

- Treat automated screening as consequential but opaque; do not claim a guaranteed workaround.
- Use conventional titles, clear chronology, and explicit qualification evidence so unconventional paths are easier to interpret.
- Omit job-irrelevant proxy signals, but do not erase legitimate international experience or alter identity.
- Encourage diversified application channels, referrals, portfolios, and human review outside the resume when the broader job-search strategy is in scope.

### 2. The New York Times: Recruiters Use A.I. to Scan Résumés. Applicants Are Trying to Trick It

Source: [The New York Times, October 7, 2025](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/07/business/ai-chatbot-prompts-resumes.html)

The article documents applicants placing hidden chatbot instructions or white text in resumes and employers adding detection. Reported recruiter responses ranged from suspicion to automatic rejection.

Resume implications:

- Ban hidden text, prompt injection, copied job descriptions, and other attempts to manipulate a model.
- Assume ATS text extraction can reveal content that visual formatting conceals.
- Make qualifications visible in ordinary language and supported bullets.

### 3. AP News: Finding a Job Is Tough. Here's How AI Can and Can't Help

Source: [Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/job-search-ai-resume-screening-interview-a535a7932ff291a1998158d40cd82c4c)

AP reports that generic AI customization can make application materials resemble everyone else's. Experts recommend specific company research and warn that there is no secret keyword that reliably defeats current systems. Employer policies commonly allow formatting, explanation, and brainstorming while prohibiting invented achievements or AI-completed assessments.

Resume implications:

- Use AI for evidence organization, comparison, editing, and targeted research—not invention.
- Tailor to actual company needs and job language instead of chasing a universal keyword score.
- Keep the final application an authentic representation of the candidate's experience and reasoning.

### 4. Forbes: AI Resumes Are Sabotaging the Hiring Process, 67% of Managers Reveal

Source: [Forbes, March 18, 2026](https://www.forbes.com/sites/rachelwells/2026/03/18/ai-resumes-are-sabotaging-the-hiring-process-67-of-managers-reveal/)

The article cites Robert Half findings that AI-heavy application volume increases recruiter workload, makes skills harder to verify, and lengthens hiring. It recommends accurate, authentic materials, role-relevant keywords, verifiable skills, a credible LinkedIn presence, and portfolio evidence.

Resume implications:

- Prefer concrete facts, named tools, scope, outcomes, and artifacts over polished generalities.
- Treat every bullet as an interview claim the candidate must be able to explain.
- Never embellish experience to resemble a job description.

### 5. BBC Worklife: AI Hiring Tools May Be Filtering Out the Best Job Applicants

Source: [BBC Worklife, February 16, 2024](https://www.bbc.com/worklife/article/20240214-ai-recruiting-hiring-software-bias-discrimination)

The article describes how systems trained on prior employees or historical hiring decisions can learn irrelevant proxies and reproduce existing patterns. Reported examples include hobby terms correlating with gender and candidates from non-standard backgrounds falling outside a model's preferred profile.

Resume implications:

- Remove unrelated hobbies and personal details that add proxy signals without qualification value.
- Make transferable skills and non-standard career transitions explicit rather than assuming inference.
- Do not claim that an algorithm is neutral merely because it is automated.

### 6. New York Post: AI Job Screeners Prefer AI-Written Resumes

Source: [New York Post, May 16, 2026](https://nypost.com/2026/05/16/us-news/artificial-intelligence-job-screeners-prefer-ai-written-resumes-over-human-ones-researchers-find/)

The article covers research on LLM self-preference: evaluators favored text produced by the same model even after content quality controls. The underlying research reports simulated shortlisting advantages of 23% to 60% for candidates whose resumes were rewritten by the evaluator's model.

Resume implications:

- Recognize model-style bias, but do not imitate generic LLM prose or guess which vendor an employer uses.
- Use standard semantic structures—clear skills, responsibilities, scale, and results—while preserving individual facts and voice.
- Do not describe AI-style matching as evidence of candidate quality.

### 7. Forbes: The AI Recruitment Takeover

Source: [Forbes, March 27, 2025](https://www.forbes.com/sites/keithferrazzi/2025/03/27/the-ai-recruitment-takeover-redefining-hiring-in-the-digital-age/)

The article describes growing use of natural-language processing, predictive analytics, skills inference, candidate ranking, and automated workflows throughout recruitment. It also identifies bias monitoring, privacy, compliance, human oversight, and accountability as necessary controls.

Resume implications:

- Optimize for semantic evidence, not keyword presence alone.
- Connect each important skill to work context and an observable result.
- Preserve human readability because ranking is only one part of a larger hiring process.

## Stable Do's

- Use one column, standard headings, conventional dates, and plain reading order.
- Match job terminology only where supported by evidence.
- Put priority skills inside accomplishment bullets, not only in a keyword list.
- State tools, scale, stakeholders, decisions enabled, and measured outcomes.
- Reconcile contradictions across source files before writing.
- Preserve the candidate's own factual voice and verify every claim.
- Use portfolios, work samples, and certifications when they strengthen verifiability.
- Keep relevant international and non-standard experience while explaining its transferability.

## Stable Don'ts

- Do not use hidden prompts, white text, metadata tricks, or pasted job descriptions.
- Do not keyword-stuff or promise a universal ATS score.
- Do not submit generic LLM language or immaculate but content-free prose.
- Do not invent skills, employment, ownership, metrics, or causation.
- Do not add irrelevant protected or proxy information.
- Do not guarantee that any resume can bypass bias, secure human review, or produce an interview.

## Derived Anti-Pattern Logic

These deductions connect the source findings to editing decisions:

1. **Generic polish increases verification cost.** When many applicants submit similarly polished text, hiring teams spend more time determining which skills and claims are real. A resume should therefore maximize verifiable detail rather than stylistic smoothness.
2. **Adjectives displace evidence.** Space used by claims such as `results-driven` or `exceptional leader` cannot simultaneously show the tool, scale, stakeholder, or result that would prove the claim.
3. **Hidden tricks create a trust failure.** Text extraction can expose white text and prompt instructions. Even if a trick changes a machine score, discovery gives a recruiter an independent reason to reject the application.
4. **Keyword stuffing weakens semantic evidence.** Modern systems increasingly infer skills from surrounding context. A priority term is more credible inside a factual accomplishment than in a repeated keyword list.
5. **Fabricated scope fails across stages.** Inflated numbers may survive document screening but often collapse under interviews, assessments, reference checks, or internal consistency review.
6. **Standard formatting reduces ambiguity.** Conventional headings and reading order help both parsers and rushed reviewers locate evidence without guessing how a designed layout should be interpreted.
7. **Bias cannot be edited away.** Removing irrelevant proxy information can reduce avoidable exposure, but no wording strategy neutralizes a biased model. Clear transferable evidence and diversified application channels are defensible; identity manipulation is not.
8. **Model self-preference is not a writing recipe.** Evidence that an evaluator may favor its own style does not reveal which model an employer uses and does not justify generic LLM prose. Candidate-specific facts remain the durable signal.

## What the Evidence Does Not Establish

The source set does not establish:

- A universal ATS keyword-density target.
- A dependable way to identify an employer's screening model.
- A wording strategy that neutralizes demographic bias.
- A reliable benefit from intentionally making prose sound like a particular LLM.
- A guarantee that an ATS-safe resume will be shortlisted.

The defensible objective is therefore not to “beat” a system. It is to produce a parseable, relevant, verifiable record of the candidate's qualifications that survives both automated and human review as well as the available evidence permits.
